# Proposed training-dose comparison

Status: a research plan for discussion, not an active training prescription.
Nothing here changes schedules, recommended loads, or recorded workouts.

## Question and comparison

Does replacing some ordinary domain work with the adaptive Chaos Machine
produce a worthwhile improvement in measured finger performance without an
unacceptable cost to climbing quality, recovery, time, or adherence?

The user-supplied Grip Gains material emphasizes climbing first, high effort,
individual training frequency, recording imperfect sessions, and avoiding more
volume than can be recovered from. It describes Kamikaze as especially taxing
and its role as unsettled. Those are the source's claims and design priorities,
not controlled evidence that either its protocol or ours maximizes adaptation.
Chaos has its own loads, rest, measurement, and adjustment rules; similar intent
does not establish that the two programs deliver the same stimulus.

Adaptive Chaos can extend later holds by reducing their targets. Hitting those
times demonstrates target attainment. It does not show that the resulting
extra work improves long-term gains. Forecast evaluation and dose evaluation
therefore need separate questions and outcomes.

## Declare the plan before collecting the comparison

An initial single-athlete feasibility design is two weeks to establish a
repeatable measurement routine, then two four-week blocks:

- Ordinary block: retain the athlete's agreed ordinary training opportunities
  and existing 4–5–6 progression. The athlete can choose another domain.
- Replacement block: keep the agreed opportunities, and replace at most one
  planned ordinary session per tested grip each week with adaptive Chaos.
  Do not add an extra session to make room for it.

Agree the block order before starting; randomize it if both orders are
practical. Prespecify which grips, ordinary opportunities, and substitutions
are being compared. An optional repeated pair of blocks would help identify
whether an apparent effect repeats. Four weeks is a feasibility choice, not a
claim that it identifies the optimal adaptation window.

Keep the intended climbing and support-work routine as comparable as practical,
and record important changes. Do not force equal time under tension or equal
exhaustion: they are consequences of the competing policies that should be
reported. Do not treat two hands as independently assigned athletes. A design
that trains both hands differently would introduce order and carryover issues
and is not the default proposal.

## Outcomes agreed in advance

Choose one primary performance outcome per tested grip before starting:
change in hold duration at a fixed, repeatable force, or change in sustained
force at a fixed duration. Choose anchors already supported by clean recorded
work, spanning a shorter and a longer duration if practical. Keep the same
device, grip, hand, position, failure criteria, preceding work, and rest for
these comparisons. Establish measurement repeatability before selecting a
minimum change worth acting on. If force is not actually maintained, do not
treat elapsed time at an unachieved target as the primary outcome.

Use the same sparse assessment schedule in both blocks, replacing work when
possible. Compare equivalent baseline and end-of-block assessments rather than
adding frequent maximal tests. Record force traces and stop reasons; interrupted
or sensor-limited tests are missing measurements, not performance losses.
Repeat a disrupted assessment under the predeclared rule rather than selecting
the better result after seeing it. Peak force over a standardized brief window
can be secondary; it does not replace the duration-specific primary outcome.

Additional outcomes:

- Climbing sessions completed, quality or effort of comparable climbing, and
  whether finger training caused valued climbing to be shortened or missed.
- Adherence, total session time, actual work, target changes, optional sets,
  and how often the athlete stops a planned session.
- Next-session comparable first-set performance, reported fatigue or symptoms,
  and unplanned reductions or additional recovery days.

The force-duration model can summarize supporting evidence, but it should not
be the sole judge of the protocol it prescribes. Preserve actual measurements,
including inconvenient results, alongside any fitted curve.

## Forecast outcomes stay separate

Freeze the evaluated model version and its pre-session forecasts. Report paired
error, bias, large misses, interval coverage where available, sample counts,
and exclusions by grip, domain, and first-versus-later session context. For
Chaos, separately score later-hold forecasts and whether adaptive targets were
reached under the recorded load and rest.

Improved forecast accuracy can justify a prediction change after independent
validation. It cannot, by itself, justify more training volume or establish
that Chaos produces better gains. Never promote a protocol simply because its
target-attainment error became smaller.

## Context and analysis rules

Save the intended protocol, immutable original prescription, actual target
changes, completed work, measured rest, stop reasons, and model/detector version.
Keep session start and hand order, grip order, and preceding climbing or support
work when known. Unknown context stays unknown. Preserve the selected and actual
protocol when a Chaos target cannot be adjusted; report those fallbacks rather
than pooling them silently with successful adaptive runs.

For sustained capacity and recovery comparisons, use comparable first sets on
separate training dates. Keep later sessions and extra sets as acute workload
and protocol-outcome evidence. Analyze independent dates and block changes;
do not inflate the sample size with reps, hands, or overlapping rolling windows.
Display raw outcomes and uncertainty alongside summaries. Record whether
missing data resulted from missed training, deliberate stopping, or equipment.

State exclusions and the minimum worthwhile gain before examining the result.
Use the agreed analysis even if an exploratory alternative looks better.
Learning, accumulated training, season changes, and carryover cannot be fully
removed in a short single-athlete comparison. Report it as a personal pilot,
not a general causal estimate or proof of an ideal program.

## Review and continuation

Review adherence and measurement quality weekly without repeatedly selecting a
winner. At each block end, assess the primary performance change against its
repeatability and practical value, with climbing cost and tolerability beside
it. Persistently worse comparable performance, compromised climbing, or adverse
symptoms justify stopping or reducing the experiment; continuing does not
depend on waiting for the recovery gauge to turn green.

Prefer the simpler or less costly policy when performance differences are
unclear. A promising result should repeat over another block and eventually
more athletes before becoming a general default. Keep new protocol versions
separate, and never rewrite old workouts to fit the new interpretation.
